\begin{abstract}
Decisions in personalized regenerative medicine manufacturing couple patient-specific
specimens with scarce reagents, idle bioreactors, and transport capacity. A
short-horizon planner can enforce these operational constraints but may overlook
costs beyond its planning window. We propose graph-aware temporal-difference
value learning to estimate those downstream costs in a synthetic network of
twenty manufacturing sites. The controller evaluates requests with the existing
manufacturing simulator, adds a learned terminal cost, and executes only the
first action before replanning. A graph convolutional network learns the cost
value from its controller's own trajectories using eight-step temporal-difference
targets, without actor training or imitation labels. We specify two planner
implementations: a finite set of fourteen state-dependent requests and
cross-entropy optimization of eight-step request sequences. Both retain
specimen transfer, reagent transshipment, idle-bioreactor relocation, and
replenishment under the original physical rules. The evaluation design separates
operational improvement over MDL-2, the contribution of the learned value over
zero-tail planning, and the graph architecture contrast against a
parameter-scale-matched multilayer perceptron. Each implementation trains ten
models across five independent blocks; independent evaluation uses fresh paired
worlds with learning disabled. This methods-stage manuscript does not yet report
completed independent results. Historical actor--critic findings are retained
separately and do not establish the superiority of the proposed method.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Personalized regenerative medicine manufacturing requires coordination between
patient-specific demand and distributed production resources. Unlike pooled
inventory, a specimen and its resulting product cannot be reassigned to another
patient. A transfer may improve access to an idle bioreactor while exposing the
specimen to additional delay. Reagent and bioreactor movements also change
which sites can serve later arrivals. Simulation-based network comparisons and
reinforcement-learning studies have examined related manufacturing and capacity
decisions~\cite{Wang2023,Tseng2025}. These studies motivate the application;
they do not validate the clinical predictions of the present simulator.

The engineering problem is to make coordinated requests while respecting
identity, availability, transport, and production constraints. The scientific
problem is more specific: can experience improve a controller beyond what an
already competent planner can achieve? A favorable comparison with an
operational heuristic does not answer this second question when the proposed
controller also changes its planning horizon or search procedure.

We address these questions with \emph{Native Value-TD}, a temporal-difference
(TD) cost-value model coupled to receding-horizon planning. ``Native'' denotes
reuse of the original twenty-site environment and its cost accounting, rather
than a reduced support-hour problem. The network predicts downstream cost;
the planner, not a neural actor, selects requests. This division lets the
simulator handle feasibility while learning addresses the truncated planning
horizon. TD learning, graph convolution, and value-augmented planning are
established components~\cite{Sutton1988,Kipf2017,Hansen2022}. The proposed
contribution is their task-specific integration with identity-preserving
manufacturing decisions and an evaluation that separates three sources of value.

We present two implementations of the common method. Variant A scores fourteen
state-dependent first-step requests followed by heuristic continuation. Variant B
uses the cross-entropy method (CEM) to search over complete residual request
sequences. B broadens the controller's decision freedom but also increases
planning computation. We do not treat an A/B difference as an isolated effect
of candidate count, or select a variant from partial training outcomes.

The manuscript therefore contributes a fully specified controller and a
prospective comparison design, not a completed demonstration of superiority.
The original deep deterministic policy gradient (DDPG) controller is retained
as a historical evaluated alternative. Its positive operational findings and
unresolved learning attribution inform the change in research direction;
they are neither erased nor presented as results for Native Value-TD.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}
\subsection{Network, identity, and operational constraints}
The benchmark contains twenty physical manufacturing sites and a fixed horizon
of $T=52$ decision epochs. Its geographic links, demand generators, production
timing, transport rules, and cost coefficients remain unchanged. The graph
representation includes the existing virtual capacity hub; its twenty-first
node is not an additional manufacturing site.

Three transfer channels are available. A waiting patient's intact specimen can
make at most one qualified direct transfer before manufacturing. The model
does not permit specimen splitting, pooling, patient reassignment, or repeated
routing. Reagents can move on the qualified resource network. Only idle
bioreactors can relocate; a batch already in production does not migrate.
The finished product remains linked to the same patient and returns to the
collection site. In the four primary conditions, specimen availability has
a one-epoch transfer lead, and finished-product return has a zero-epoch lead.
These are modeling assumptions, not evidence that real transport is instantaneous
or clinically safe.

Let $x_t$ denote the complete simulator state and $o_t$ the information available
to the controller. Public information includes inventories, production and
transfer pipelines, demand observations and history, supplier status, patient
summaries, and time. The controller must not access the realized future demand
or the true latent patient attributes as a prediction oracle. Its feature
tensor $X_t=\phi(o_t)$ is a summary, not a demonstrated sufficient statistic
for a fully observed Markov decision process.

\subsection{Requests, execution, and objective}
At each epoch the controller issues an eighty-dimensional normalized request,
\begin{equation}
 a_t=(a_t^{\mathrm{s}},a_t^{\mathrm{r}},a_t^{\mathrm{b}},
 a_t^{\mathrm{q}})\in[-1,1]^{80},
 \qquad a_t^{g}\in[-1,1]^{20}.
 \label{eq:action}
\end{equation}
The groups request specimen transfer, reagent transfer, idle-bioreactor
relocation, and reagent replenishment, respectively. Transfer coordinates
encode site-level net requests, not independently selectable flows on every
edge. Replenishment coordinates are mapped to nonnegative order quantities
subject to the original supplier and quantity limits. The environment decodes
these requests into feasible operations using its unchanged availability,
integrality, network, and patient-identity rules. Thus unrestricted sampling
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:action} is not unrestricted control of every physical flow.

The one-epoch cost retains the original decomposition,
\begin{equation}
 c_t=c_t^{\mathrm{operating}}+
 c_t^{\mathrm{patient\ loss}}+c_t^{\mathrm{expiry}}+
 c_t^{\mathrm{urgency}}, \qquad r_t=-c_t.
 \label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
Operating cost already contains the charged transfer components; those
components must not be added again when explaining total cost. Costs are
modeled weighted-objective units, not realized monetary savings. For a
controller $\pi$, the objective is
\begin{equation}
 J(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}c_t\right].
 \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
The discount factor is one. Patient losses, completions, and unresolved
obligations at epoch 52 are reported alongside cost. No extra settlement
period or lifetime-cost objective is introduced. In particular, a zero value
at $T$ denotes the endpoint of this experiment, not the absence of outstanding
patient needs.

\section{Proposed Method: Native Value-TD}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Short-horizon planning with a learned cost value}
Let $V_\theta(X_t)$ estimate the cost remaining from epoch $t$ to $T$ under
the current receding-horizon controller. It estimates \emph{cost}, so smaller
values are preferred. At a decision epoch, the planner considers a request
or request-sequence candidate $z$ and simulates $h_t=\min(8,T-t)$ epochs in
$K=2$ sampled prediction futures. Its score is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat J_t(z)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}
 \left[\sum_{j=0}^{h_t-1}\widehat c_{t+j}^{(k)}(z)
 +\mathbf{1}_{\{t+h_t<T\}}V_\theta
       (\widehat X_{t+h_t}^{(k)}(z))\right].
 \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
Hatted quantities are predictions, not realized future observations. The
controller executes only the first request of the selected candidate,
observes the next state, and solves a new planning problem. The learned
term approximates consequences beyond the explicit eight-step window.
Native H8 uses the same planner with $V\equiv0$. A-H8 and B-H8 denote the
zero-tail versions of their respective search procedures.

Prediction clones use the existing nonprivileged conditional patient sampler
and observable-demand estimator. The sampler conditions on observed patient
age, survival, and risk category; latent patient attributes are resampled
from a fixed synthetic prior. The implementation uses a 40,000-particle prior
with survival evaluations at seventeen ages. It resets hidden demand-regime
and disruption state rather than exposing the realized simulator future.
Both futures are restored to the same initial states and random-stream starts
for every candidate at that decision, including both CEM rounds in B.
These common random numbers support within-decision comparison; they do not
make the predictions exact or establish externally calibrated patient risk.

The original MDL-2 rule supplies an operational anchor using public state.
It is not the four-site fixed-support policy. It remains a standalone
baseline and also supplies continuation or sequence anchors inside the
planner. Consequently, Native Value-TD adds learning and planning to an
existing operational rule; it does not learn the entire manufacturing
process from an unconstrained action interface.

\subsection{Graph and flat cost-value models}
\label{sec:representation}
Both learned models consume the same normalized $X_t\in\mathbb{R}^{21\times36}$
tensor. It contains the original operational and patient-summary features,
adaptive-demand features, time, and public MDL-2 action features. Normalization
uses configuration-derived scales, not statistics estimated from test worlds.
The graph uses the configured information, specimen, resource, and
capacity-hub connectivity and its prescribed edge weights.

Write $A$ for the weighted symmetric adjacency, $\widetilde A=A+I$, and
$\widehat A=\widetilde D^{-1/2}\widetilde A\widetilde D^{-1/2}$, where
$\widetilde D$ contains the row sums of $\widetilde A$. The implemented
two-layer graph encoder is
\begin{equation}
 H^{(0)}=X_t,\qquad
 H^{(\ell+1)}=\operatorname{ReLU}\!\left(
 \widehat A[H^{(\ell)}W_\ell+\mathbf{1}b_\ell^\top]\right),
 \quad \ell=0,1.
 \label{eq:gcn}
\end{equation}
Each hidden layer has width 64. A linear scalar head consumes the flattened
node embeddings,
\begin{equation}
 v_\theta(X_t)=w^\top\vecop(H^{(2)})+b,
 \qquad V_\theta(X_t)=10^9v_\theta(X_t).
 \label{eq:value}
\end{equation}
The factor $10^9$ changes numerical units only. This is a learned full
cost-to-go estimate, not a learned residual added to a fixed heuristic tail.
The output head is initially zero, and its output is not constrained to
be nonnegative after training.

The flat comparator applies a multilayer perceptron with ReLU hidden widths
10 and 24 to $\vecop(X_t)$, followed by the same scalar-output convention.
The graph and flat networks have 7,873 and 7,859 trainable parameters,
respectively, a gap below 0.2\%. This controls parameter scale and input
information, not architecture or floating-point operation count. Graph
convolution shares transformations between neighboring nodes, whereas the
flat model learns global connections directly. The flattened graph readout
is tied to the fixed node ordering; we do not claim a permutation-invariant
controller or demonstrated transfer to unseen network sizes.

\subsection{Observed-trajectory temporal-difference learning}
\label{sec:td}
Each controller collects its own complete 52-epoch trajectory while holding
its value parameters fixed during that trajectory. Let $\theta^-$ denote
the parameters immediately before the episode's training updates and
$u_t=\min(t+n,T)$ with $n=8$. The normalized TD target is
\begin{equation}
 y_t=\sg\!\left[
 \frac{1}{10^9}\sum_{j=t}^{u_t-1}c_j+
 \mathbf{1}_{\{u_t<T\}}v_{\theta^-}(X_{u_t})
 \right].
 \label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
The costs and states in Eq.~\eqref{eq:target} are observed along the
controller's actual simulated trajectory, not counterfactual planner labels.
The stop-gradient operator $\sg$ holds all target values fixed throughout
that episode's update block. For a minibatch $\mathcal B$, the loss is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L(\theta)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal B|}
 \sum_{t\in\mathcal B}[v_\theta(X_t)-y_t]^2.
 \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
After each completed episode, the learner performs 32 Adam updates at
learning rate $10^{-4}$, sampling batches of 64 transitions with replacement
from that episode alone. The target snapshot is recomputed at the next
episode, not updated by a Polyak moving average. There is no cross-episode
replay, actor update, imitation target, pretraining, reward search,
early-success stopping, or best-checkpoint selection.

This is value-based reinforcement learning combined with model-based action
selection: experience supplies the cost targets, TD bootstrapping estimates
later consequences, and replanning converts the learned value into decisions.
It is not DQN, because it learns a state cost value rather than a discrete
action-value table. It is not an actor--critic algorithm, because no actor
is optimized. Training occurs in simulation; all parameters remain frozen
during independent evaluation. The initial zero head makes each learned
controller behaviorally identical to its own H8 counterpart under matched
planning random streams, although the neural implementation incurs extra
computation.

\subsection{Variant A: finite request rollout}
\label{sec:variant-a}
Variant A reuses fourteen state-dependent request templates. Let
$a^{(2)}(o_t)$ be MDL-2 and $a^{(3)}(o_t)$ the same heuristic configured
with a three-period lookahead. Four specimen corrections, with signed
magnitudes $-0.10,-0.05,0.05,0.10$, are applied separately around each
anchor. Two reagent corrections and two combined reagent/idle-bioreactor
corrections, each with signed magnitude $\pm0.05$, are added around MDL-2.
The count is therefore
\begin{equation}
 |\mathcal A_t|_{\mathrm{list}}=2+2\times4+2+2=14.
\end{equation}
The corrections follow centered on-hand imbalance patterns computed from
the current public state. Appendix~\ref{app:candidates} gives their precise
definition. ``Four specimen corrections'' means two magnitudes in two
directions, not four destinations or four individual patients.

The fourteen entries are positions in a candidate list, not fourteen
fixed physical actions; clipping and feasibility can make entries
behaviorally identical. After each candidate's first predicted action,
MDL-2 supplies the remaining $h_t-1$ actions. The minimum score in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:score} selects the executed request; a gain no greater than
$10^{-6}$ over MDL-2 falls back to MDL-2. Replenishment follows the selected
anchor. Capacity corrections are coupled to reagent corrections in this
class. The method retains all physical transfer channels but does not
independently optimize all request coordinates. Fourteen is a frozen
engineering design, not a theoretically sufficient or optimal action count.

\subsection{Variant B: sampled residual-sequence planning}
\label{sec:variant-b}
Variant B broadens the search using CEM~\cite{DeBoer2005}. It optimizes
an eight-step residual sequence $R\in[-2,2]^{8\times80}$. In prediction
future $k$, its action at step $j$ is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat a_{t+j}^{(k)}=
 \clip\!\left(a^{(2)}(\widehat o_{t+j}^{(k)})+R_j,-1,1\right).
 \label{eq:sequence}
\end{equation}
The same $R$ is scored in both futures. The public-state anchor can respond
to each predicted state, but there is no future-specific optimization of
$R$. The first anchor uses the live public state. Because each normalized
anchor coordinate lies in $[-1,1]$, residual bounds of $[-2,2]$ can represent
any normalized request coordinate after clipping. This representational
property does not imply that finite sampling finds a good or globally
optimal sequence.

CEM uses two rounds with 32 candidate positions per round: a zero sequence,
the incumbent, and thirty Gaussian proposals. It retains the eight
lowest-score elites, updates the mean and standard deviation with equal
weight on old and elite statistics, and enforces a standard-deviation
floor of 0.1. Initial standard deviation is 0.5; Gaussian innovations
have temporal AR(1) correlation 0.5 and independent request coordinates
before clipping. The zero sequence receives the same $10^{-6}$ tie
preference as the anchor, and the global incumbent changes only for a
strict improvement beyond that tolerance.

After selecting a sequence, the controller executes its first request,
shifts the residual sequence forward, and appends a zero row as the next
warm start. Standard deviation resets at every decision; all warm-start
state resets at a new world. There is no additional final rescoring.
Table~\ref{tab:planners} summarizes the distinction from A. Graph, Flat,
and H8 share the CEM algorithm, initial random streams, and allocation.
Their later candidate distributions can differ because the value term
changes elite selection. The learned-tail contrast therefore includes
the search adaptation induced by that value.

\subsection{Training and control procedure}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:method} specifies the shared training loop. Five
independent blocks are trained per variant. Within each block, graph and
flat models receive matched exogenous training-world starts, but generate
their own trajectories and targets. Each model completes 96 worlds,
cycling through the four conditions to give 24 worlds per condition.
This yields 3,072 updates per model and 30,720 updates per variant.
Model initialization, world generation, prediction, and CEM search streams
are recorded separately. A and B use independent training allocations.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Native Value-TD training and frozen evaluation}
\label{alg:method}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Fixed environment, variant A or B, architecture, allocated seeds
\State Initialize encoder parameters and zero scalar value head
\For{each of the 96 allocated training worlds}
  \State Reset world and planner; hold $\theta$ fixed during collection
  \For{$t=0,\ldots,51$}
    \State Construct public features $X_t$ and two prediction futures
    \State Score A requests or B sequences using Eq.~\eqref{eq:score}
    \State Execute the selected first request in the original environment
    \State Store observed $(X_t,c_t,X_{t+1})$
  \EndFor
  \State Compute the fixed TD targets in Eq.~\eqref{eq:target}
  \For{32 updates}
    \State Sample 64 current-world transitions with replacement
    \State Minimize Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss} with one Adam update
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State Seal the final parameter state without checkpoint selection
\State After the all-model barrier, evaluate on allocated fresh worlds
\State Keep parameters frozen and record cost, service, harms, and compute
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Rationale and limits of the approximation}
\label{sec:rationale}
The theoretical motivation is the finite-horizon return decomposition: for
a fixed policy, full information state, and exact dynamics, the expected
remaining cost equals expected cost over the next $h$ steps plus the
policy's value at the resulting endpoint. TD learning approximates the
latter quantity from experience~\cite{Sutton1988}. This identity motivates
a learned tail; it is not a convergence theorem for the present nonlinear,
partially observed controller.

Three structural advantages are relevant to this application. First,
request evaluation runs through the existing physical simulator, so the
value learner need not differentiate through discrete patient routing or
integer resource execution. Second, the neural model predicts one scalar
downstream cost rather than learning both an action map and an action
critic. Third, graph message passing exposes neighborhood relationships
before the cost readout, providing an inductive bias for network coupling.
These properties explain the design choice, not a demonstrated performance
ranking against DDPG or the flat model.

The design also incurs limitations. Two sampled futures approximate rather
than integrate prediction uncertainty. A restricts first-step choices and
uses MDL-2 continuation, whereas its learned tail estimates the
receding-horizon controller; this is approximate rollout improvement, not
an exact Bellman-optimal policy. B reduces this action-class restriction
but its short CEM search can miss useful sequences. Its prediction-step
allocation is $64/14\approx4.57$ times A's, excluding other overhead.
Neither equal training-world counts nor equal parameter counts imply
equal computation or latency.

The planning horizon $h=8$ and TD span $n=8$ are distinct fixed parameters:
one controls explicit lookahead, the other the bootstrap target. Their
equality does not establish that eight is optimal. Shorter or longer
horizons change model error, tail dependence, target variance, and cost
of computation. A future sensitivity study would need separately
allocated development data and prospective evaluation; the present
experiments do not select these settings from test performance.

\section{Why the Primary Method Changed from DDPG}
\label{sec:ddpg}
DDPG is a model-free actor--critic method for continuous actions
\cite{Lillicrap2016}. It learns an action map and a critic, and updates
the actor through the critic's action gradient. It also uses TD learning;
the change is therefore not from ``non-TD'' to ``TD.'' It is from direct
actor optimization to cost-value learning with explicit planning.

In our historical routing-primary controller, the actor learned bounded
specimen-routing residuals around MDL-2, while resource decisions remained
anchored. The historical pipeline also used advantage-filtered pretraining.
Its saved comparisons supported operational improvements, but did not
establish that the subsequent actor--critic updates added an independent
endpoint benefit over the frozen pretrained policy. Appendix~\ref{app:history}
retains both findings. That attribution gap motivates a learning rule
based on the controller's own observed costs and a direct zero-tail
planning comparator.

The constrained request interface supplies a second motivation. Clipping,
integer execution, and availability limits can map different continuous
requests to the same physical operation. A learned action-gradient
surrogate must account for this mapping; explicit simulation instead
scores its executed consequences. This is an engineering rationale,
not a proof that DDPG cannot handle the task. DDPG retains the practical
advantage of inexpensive actor-only action selection, whereas Native
Value-TD pays for repeated simulator predictions.

The historical controller is thus archived as an evaluated method that
is no longer the principal proposal, not removed from the scientific
record or declared universally inferior. The current A/B protocol does
not include a matched, contemporaneous DDPG arm. Differences between
historical and current results cannot establish a TD-versus-DDPG treatment
effect. Similarly, although our terminal-value design is related to
TD-MPC~\cite{Hansen2022}, we use the existing manufacturing model rather
than jointly learned latent dynamics and a learned actor. B is native-model
CEM planning with TD value learning, not a reproduction of TD-MPC.

\section{Experimental Design and Evidence Status}
\label{sec:experiments}
\subsection{Conditions, models, and independent worlds}
The four original conditions are nominal demand with history, an abrupt
demand-regime shift, regional demand drift, and compound regional stress.
The frozen scenario configurations, including all original physical and
cost rules, define these conditions. Neither a reduced network nor a
more favorable demand or reward setting substitutes for this benchmark.

Variant A allocates $5\times2\times96=960$ training trajectories.
All ten final learned models must be sealed before independent evaluation.
Its test set contains $5\times4\times12=240$ fresh worlds, each evaluated
by MDL-2, A-H8, A-Graph, and A-Flat, giving 960 evaluation trajectories.
Variant B independently allocates the same 960 training trajectories.
After all B models and all original A final models are sealed, B evaluates
240 additional fresh worlds with seven roles: MDL-2, B-H8, B-Graph,
B-Flat, A-H8, A-Graph, and A-Flat, giving 1,680 evaluation trajectories.
A's final weights are reused unchanged; its earlier test worlds are not.
B's new synthetic prior and prediction streams are shared across these
seven roles. Thus B's A/B comparison is prospective within its new test
worlds, not a merger of unmatched test summaries.

\subsection{Outcomes and descriptive support rule}
All comparisons retain every assigned condition, block, and completed
world, including adverse outcomes. We report mean total cost and its
additive components, patient losses and completions, unresolved patients
at cutoff, transfer activity, and computation. With $\overline C_p$ and
$\overline C_q$ denoting equal-condition mean costs of a candidate and
comparator, cost saving is
\begin{equation}
 S_{p:q}=100\left(1-\frac{\overline C_p}{\overline C_q}\right)\%,
 \qquad \Delta L_{p:q}=\overline L_p-\overline L_q.
 \label{eq:effects}
\end{equation}
This ratio of means is not the mean of individual-world percentage
changes. Positive $S$ and negative $\Delta L$ favor the candidate.

The frozen descriptive screen requires mean cost saving of at least
0.5\%, positive mean saving in each of five blocks, a positive lower
bound of the descriptive 95\% cost interval, and nonpositive mean extra
patient losses overall and separately in every condition. Uncertainty
uses 2,000 hierarchical bootstrap draws with common block resampling
across conditions and independent within-block, within-condition world
resampling. All six A role pairs and all twenty-one B role pairs remain
in the readout. The screen is not a multiplicity-adjusted significance
test, and a favorable mean does not imply that no individual world is
harmed or that clinical noninferiority has been established.

\subsection{Computation, reproducibility, and current status}
Implementation uses CPU float32 value models. Training exposure and
update counts are fixed before evaluation, with no test-time updates.
The preserved records bind source and configuration hashes, seed
allocations, raw trajectories, optimizer states, prediction/search random
states, final model hashes, and admitted versus completed operation counts.
The result readers reconcile saved costs and patient flows independently
of new environment or model execution. Failed attempts and abandoned
partial work remain in the provenance record.

\textbf{Independent results are pending in this revision.} The running
campaigns and partial training summaries do not establish any of the
contrasts in Table~\ref{tab:contrasts}. Final effect estimates, condition-wise
harms, measured latency, and archive-verified completion must be added
from the completed readouts. We do not report interim training savings
as out-of-sample results or use them to revise the frozen method.
Historical four-site support-hour studies are separate experiments and
provide no efficacy evidence for the twenty-site controller described here.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
Native Value-TD assigns learning a specific role: estimating consequences
that a bounded planner does not explicitly simulate. This separation makes
the operational mechanism inspectable and permits a meaningful H8
comparator. It does not make the learned value accurate by construction.
Approximation error can change candidate rankings, and the planner can
favor states whose downstream cost is underestimated.

The graph architecture provides structured parameter sharing but does not
guarantee an advantage. A flat model may represent the relevant global
relationships adequately in a fixed twenty-site network. A graph benefit
must therefore be established in the same controller and test worlds as
the learning benefit. A positive result from one experiment cannot be
combined with a different experiment's learning result to support both
contributions for a single method.

The model remains synthetic and same-generator. Patient deterioration,
supplier disruptions, transport timing, and eligibility rules are not
prospectively calibrated to a clinical deployment. The finite 52-epoch
objective leaves unresolved obligations, and the controller uses approximate
public-state summaries. External validity, network-size transfer,
horizon sensitivity, broader safety constraints, and compute-matched
search comparisons remain open questions. These limitations also rule
out claims of guaranteed deployment benefit or clinical safety.

\section{Conclusion}
We specify graph-aware TD cost-value learning for receding-horizon
planning in the original twenty-site manufacturing network. The method
retains the native physical constraints and separates learned downstream
cost from explicit request search. Variants A and B expose a concrete
trade-off between a restricted, inexpensive candidate class and broader,
more costly sequence optimization. DDPG remains a preserved historical
alternative rather than the primary proposal. Whether the new method
improves operations, whether TD learning contributes beyond H8, and
whether graph structure contributes beyond a flat model are separate
empirical questions awaiting the prescribed independent evaluation.

\section{Definition of the Fourteen Request Templates}
\label{app:candidates}
For a vector $z\in\mathbb{R}^{20}$, define
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal N(z)=\frac{z-\bar z\mathbf1}
 {\max\{\max_i|z_i-\bar z|,10^{-6}\}}.
\end{equation}
Let $d,f,s,r,b,p$ denote
current demand, forecast demand, waiting specimens, on-hand reagents,
idle bioreactors, and pending inbound specimens. Let $q$ be the sum of
the exposed at-risk and near-expiry counts. The existing option generator
uses
\begin{align}
 p^{\mathrm{s}}&=\mathcal N(\min(r,b)-s-p),\\
 p^{\mathrm{r}}&=\mathcal N(d+0.25f+s-r+0.5q),\\
 p^{\mathrm{b}}&=\mathcal N(d+0.25f+s-b+0.5q).
\end{align}
Here the minimum is coordinatewise. A specimen correction adds
$\epsilon p^{\mathrm{s}}$ to the specimen request group of an anchor,
with $\epsilon\in\{-0.10,-0.05,0.05,0.10\}$. Reagent corrections add
$\epsilon p^{\mathrm{r}}$ to the reagent group with $\epsilon=\pm0.05$.
Combined corrections add the latter and $\epsilon p^{\mathrm{b}}$ to
the capacity group. The complete request is clipped to $[-1,1]$.
These coefficients specify a reused heuristic candidate generator;
they are not new reward weights or learned neural parameters.

\section{Historical DDPG Record and Scope Separation}
\label{app:history}
The retained routing-primary evidence synthesis covers five training
seeds and 2,000 paired formal outcomes per comparison. It records
0.658\% lower mean cost for the graph controller than MDL-2 and
0.321\% lower mean cost than its implemented flat-DDPG comparator.
Mean patient losses were lower by 25.263 and 16.375 per world,
respectively. However, the final graph policy's cost was 0.00273\%
higher than the frozen pretrained policy's point estimate. The compact
top-level summary does not store an interval for that final-versus-frozen
estimate. The historical result therefore supports an integrated
controller comparison, not a separately established gain from subsequent
actor--critic training.

Transport sensitivity was not uniformly favorable: under zero-epoch
specimen availability the graph controller cost 0.633\% more than MDL-2,
whereas a one-epoch finished-product return sensitivity favored it by
1.050\%. These results remain part of the record. The historical
graph-versus-flat package is not retrospectively relabeled a pure
edge-causality experiment, and the evaluated restricted controller must
not be confused with all action groups allowed by later configurations.

The local archival index, \texttt{archive/ddpg-method-record.md}, links
the full historical method and evidence synthesis. The pre-revision
four-site support-hour manuscript is preserved separately under
\texttt{archive/2026-10-11-pre-native-value-td/}. None of these historical
outcomes is pooled with the current A/B evaluation.
